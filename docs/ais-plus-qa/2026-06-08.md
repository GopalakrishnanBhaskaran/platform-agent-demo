## Q&A

### 1. Cheryl: Which LLM is best for drafting email replies in my voice? I've spent two days on it and it keeps reusing my old emails.
- **Model:** Nate always uses Claude models for writing. Sonnet is enough, and you don't need Opus.
- **Break it down and prove one thing first:** that a draft sounds like you.
  - Analyse your past emails and pick good examples, then put them in one markdown "system prompt".
  - Add a vector database of past emails only if you truly need it.
- **Decide the end state, then work back.** A triage-and-draft system and a full email executive assistant are very different builds.
  - For drafting, her Cloudflare setup is fine.
  - For an assistant whose knowledge grows, an out-of-the-box agent with heartbeats (OpenClaw or Hermes) fits better.
  - n8n would also work, but isn't worth learning just for this.

### 2. Moe: What's the best way to write new LinkedIn posts from six months of past ones?
- **Nate's setup:** a LinkedIn-writing skill that references a markdown style guide. The guide covers how he talks, phrases he never uses, and AI-sounding sentence patterns to avoid.
- **The skill learns:** after each post he feeds back his edits and asks it to update the skill with why he changed them.
- **Steps:**
  1. Pick your top ~10 posts *and* some you dislike or that flopped. Bad examples teach a lot.
  2. Have Claude analyse them (plus research on hooks and CTAs) into a style guide. Brief it like a new intern hired only to write your posts.
  3. Build the skill on top. Nate's researches the topic, proposes 3–4 angles, and drafts three posts with different hooks for him to pick and refine.
- He has three LinkedIn skills: blog to post, YouTube video to post, and a rough thought to post.

### 3. Mike: My VS Code Explorer has hundreds of projects and markdown files. Some skills are needed everywhere, some only in one project. How do I keep it from getting cluttered?
- **Nate's answer: one operating system project that drills down into everything,** with a folder called `Other Worlds` for his other Claude Code projects.
  - He can `cd` into any of them, and the OS still has a high-level view.
  - Nobody knows the best practice yet.
- **His test:** at what point does it stop finding information you know is there, or stop invoking the right skills and sub-agents?
- **Two requirements:**
  1. It makes sense to you: you could click through and find things yourself.
  2. Routing rules tell the agent where things live ("for X, search this folder").
- If it burns input tokens reading everything instead of going straight to the source, the routing needs work. He has Claude lint and reorganise regularly.
- **One folder makes multi-device easy.** He pushes the OS to GitHub and pulls it on his laptop, where he used to push six separate projects.

### 4. Imraan: I'm an accountant, not a developer. How do I rebuild apps I made in Zite and Base44 in Claude Code? And where should I start with my OS?
- Nate has built internal tools and demos, but never an app at scale, so take this with a grain of salt.
- **Make Claude Code your head developer.** Give it personas or sub-agents: front-end, back-end, QA and a project manager.
- **Bring in expertise you don't have.** Spend a full day having agents research front-end, back-end, databases, user logins and secrets, and condense it into an expert markdown guide.
- Have agents stress-test the system. For peace of mind, hire a freelancer to QA the final app. Beware "dark code" you don't understand.
- **The OS:** start using Claude Code today for everything you'd do in Claude chat. An OS isn't built in a day; it grows daily as you connect tools and build memory.

### 5. Sahaj: I'm 22 with e-commerce credibility (Business Insider press). I can build, but I'm stuck testing offers and targeting and can't book calls.
- **Treat the old credibility as worthless for getting in the door.** It's a different space. Lead with what your automations have done; mention the rest once you're on the call.
- **"Proven offers" mislead.** The same offer works for one person and not another because of the lead list or the proof. See Nate's "six automations businesses are buying" video.
- **Jumping between offers every few weeks probably hurts.**
  - Get a foot in the door with something easy to say yes to, such as consulting hours (see "the offer you can sell tomorrow morning").
  - Turn that relationship into a project, then target people who look like your first success. That's how your niche finds you.

### 6. Jefkins: How do I populate my knowledge layer when I'm just starting? When does a business need a VPS?
- **Knowledge layer:**
  1. Follow the AIOS course's buckets (the tools you use for customers, revenue and comms).
  2. Connect your most-used sources (Gmail, ClickUp, Slack).
  3. Run the **grill-me** skill: "I want you to understand everything about my business and the customer journey, top to bottom of funnel." After 15–45 questions it has mapped the business.
- **Start with markdown files in layer folders** (Jefkins has governance, knowledge, engineering and business). Look at a knowledge graph or LLM wiki only once it grows. Markdown is cheap for agents to read.
- If it keeps reading a big file but only needs the first 20 lines, split those lines out.
- **VPS:** when something must run in the cloud (local models, a hosted OpenClaw) and you don't want to buy hardware, or you need a siloed, secure environment. That's relevant for regulated work like his health-care niche.
- **One OS file for Claude and Codex?** Yes, he's on the right path. Shared files work for any agent. Only the tool-specific files (`CLAUDE.md`, `.claude/`, `AGENTS.md`, `.agents/`) need keeping in sync. Nate has a video on making projects work in both.

### 7. Fedor: An event agency wants payment instructions, calendar confirmations and team to-dos triggered automatically, but they manage clients in Gmail threads. Do they need a CRM first?
- **Yes, they need one source of truth.** Each automation needs a clean trigger.
- Build it as a chronological SOP. Make step 1 work 100 times out of 100, then feed its output into step 2.
- **Google Sheets** works and is free and quick, but a real CRM saves a painful migration later.
- Nate's team runs its whole CRM on **GoHighLevel**: legacy, well-documented, with built-in automations.

### 8. Yury: My repo is bloated with temp files and generated artifacts (1–1.5 GB). How do I clean up safely?
- **Nate's agent can't delete anything.** It moves files to an archive folder, organised by month.
- **Archives are gitignored.** Nate deletes them after a few months, or moves them to a cloud drive first.
- **Block the agent from reading the archive** with a read-deny rule in settings. It can still move files in, but won't waste tokens reading them.
- No special cleanup agents. He keeps it simple and deletes temp files by hand.

### 9. Mohamed: I've cold-emailed and cold-called UK estate agents for two months with lead-response and follow-up systems. They're not interested. Pivot the offer or the niche?

- **Lead lists are half the battle.** See the Savon interview on YouTube. Check your warm network for introductions before anything else.
- Pick a niche as a beginner only if you have a real connection to it. Otherwise start broad.

### 19. Dong: For an Obsidian second brain, do you keep separate brains per company? How do you sync them?
- **Nate keeps everything in one.**
- **The exception is client data.** For a workspace shared with a client, under NDA, or holding sensitive data:
  - Use a completely standalone project.
  - Block your other projects' agents from reading it. An agent might grep your whole file system and read or document things you never intended.
- **The principle:** if an agent can read or touch something, assume it will, even unasked.
- **The permission layer isn't prompts.** It's deterministic permissions, API keys, environments, rules and restrictions.

## Key takeaways
1. **Prove one piece at a time.** For a voice-matched email drafter, first prove the voice with a markdown style guide and good and bad examples.
2. **Writing skills should learn.** Feed your edits back and have the skill update itself.
3. **Keep one OS folder that drills into everything** (with an `Other Worlds` folder for side projects). It's intuitive to you, routed for the agent, and one GitHub repo syncs it across devices.
4. **Use grill-me to map your business** into the knowledge layer. Markdown is enough until it isn't.
5. **Archive, don't delete,** and block agents from reading archives.
6. **Store a team's skills where the team will look** (often Notion). Adoption beats tech.
7. **Keep client and NDA data in standalone projects.** Enforce boundaries with deterministic permissions, not prompts.
8. **Sell outcomes or hours until a system is proven.** Cold outreach takes about 10× the volume you expect.
