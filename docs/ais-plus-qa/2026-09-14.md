# AIS+ Q&A: Sep 14, 2026

**Host:** Nate Herk · **Length:** 67 min · **Source:** Fireflies

## Q&A

### 1. Cheryl: Should an AI avatar interviewer act like a real host, and should you disclose that it's AI?
- **It depends on the industry and your audience.** In some industries people would think an AI interview is cool. In others, like booking physical therapy, an avatar would just hurt the experience.
- **Always disclose that it's AI.** If you disclose it and the conversation feels real-time, most people won't mind.
- For now Nate would pick a **voice agent over an avatar**. Avatars still feel less real-time and "cheaper", and a slow one frustrates people.
- Ask the first 20–30 people for feedback: did they like it or hate it?
- **Stack:** an ElevenLabs voice agent, or Retell.
- **Follow-up: What about an AI avatar co-host for a new YouTube/podcast channel?** It's not unethical, especially in the AI niche, because you're still the human driving the conversation. A fully faceless channel run by an avatar would turn more people off.
- It works like a live "grill me" skill. The AI keeps asking questions and can draw out stories or perspectives you wouldn't share on your own.
- Let the data decide. For example, do 5 episodes with a human guest, 5 with the avatar and 5 solo, then compare engagement.
- **Follow-up: Should I use HeyGen?** Nate hasn't used real-time avatars. Look at HeyGen, but research other real-time options before committing.

### 2. Rashauna: My support assistant works on 8 clean chunks with a guessed threshold. What breaks between that demo and a first real install?
- Nate assumed a vector database with a confidence threshold. Rashauna couldn't confirm live.
- **Human inputs are the riskiest part** because they're the least predictable. Test edge cases, such as a user cursing at the bot or asking about something outside the 8 chunks. Does it hallucinate?
- **Skipping evals is where things break.** A 5-question demo doesn't prove it works at scale.
- **Log every execution**, failures included: the trigger, the inputs, what the agent did and the output. A Google Sheet is enough.
- Messy docs that aren't ingested well can cause hallucinations or conflicts, even with only a few chunks.
- **Pricing without a case study:** before the scope of work, agree on an objective definition of done and **one KPI**. Example: cut support time from 5 hours/week to 1 hour/week within 90 days of going live.
- Put that KPI in the scope of work so the client can't later claim they got no value.

### 3. Maheshwar: My AIOS keeps growing and the CLAUDE.md "director" file is getting big. How should I handle it?
- Watch Nate's videos "steal my exact AIOS setup" and "every level of AI second brain". They cover data routing and when to use wikis, context files, connections or a vector database.
- Nate still runs the structure from those videos: a few wikis and multiple projects. His test is whether it finds things fast and knows where to look.
- Teach it to notice gaps. If it finds nothing locally, it should check sources like ClickUp or Fireflies before saying "I don't know."
- **Build pipelines so accuracy doesn't rely on human consistency.** One of Nate's automations adds Fireflies transcripts every day. Another deletes old ones each quarter.
- When it gets something wrong, have it retrace its steps: where did it look and what did it search? Tell it why that was wrong and where it should have looked, then have it update CLAUDE.md.
- **Follow-up: How do I keep a work and a home computer in sync when `.env` doesn't go through GitHub?** Sync the repo through GitHub and send `.env` separately through a password manager (LastPass, 1Password). Nate doesn't use a VPS for this.
- Email or ClickUp would probably work, but it isn't best practice. A member suggested the 1Password CLI integration, which Nate plans to try.

### 4. Tyler: What's the best setup for traveling when you can't bring your main machine?
- Before a trip, Nate pushes his project to GitHub, pulls it onto his laptop and copies over his `.env` file. Most of his data lives in OneDrive or Google Drive.
- He packs a mouse, a keyboard and a portable monitor to make laptop work less painful.
- For resources that only exist locally, use a remote-access tool such as Tailscale to control the home machine. It has to stay on.
- Nate leaves his PC on with the Claude and Codex desktop apps open. He can remote in or have them send him a local file.
- The risk: Tyler's always-on machine once crashed after 5 days, and another time it lost power.
- **Follow-up (Holger): Why not use a virtual machine you can copy and take with you?** It's another option. Nate once ran his AIOS on a VPS so he could talk to it from apps like Telegram, but he shut it down because he didn't get much value from it. Some people love that setup.

### 5. Holger: What should go in my portfolio, should it live on GitHub or a website, and should I find clients locally or online?
- **Which builds:** the ones that most closely solve the prospect's problem. A great lead-gen build won't win a client who needs customer support help.
- Anyone can build an impressive-looking portfolio in a day now, so yours has to be specific and outcome-driven. Clients care about outcomes, not which tools you used.
- **GitHub or website:** a website. Most clients find GitHub intimidating. Have the demos ready to pull up on calls.
- **Local or online:** both have trade-offs. Local work lets you pitch in person and do a free project for a strong case study. Online opens up a much bigger market.
- Living in South America without speaking the local language, Nate would look for clients online, starting with his warm network and Upwork.

### 6. Anon: For a website build, do you work from a local folder or the cloud?

- He tests changes on localhost before pushing them to the live site.
- Because the repo auto-deploys, he can update the site while traveling.

### 7. Jeff: A client won't sign a contract after months of work on a handshake deal. Have you ever fired a client?
- Yes, but never over conflict. It was always poor communication, which slows the work and hurts results.
- Nate's contracts now list **client expectations and requirements**: deadlines for API keys, the golden data set and system access, plus a one-hour weekly call.
- They also include a **ghosting clause**. If the client doesn't respond within, say, 20 days, Nate can terminate. Payments already made aren't refunded, and the remaining milestones are cancelled.
- If it could become a SaaS, the work isn't wasted, because it solves a problem many businesses have.
- **Next step:** one more call. Bring written communication standards, propose a weekly standing call, ask what success looks like to them, add milestone payments and get it signed.
- **Follow-up: Should I just walk away?** Give it one more shot to formalize the handshake. If they won't, part ways.

### 8. Abdulhamid: My WhatsApp agent video got 200K views and brought in clients. Which 5 systems should I build for free in exchange for testimonials?
- **Double down on WhatsApp agents**, since that's what the algorithm rewarded. Follow the data, like Nate did with n8n.
- One agent becomes several demos by changing the prompts and the database: customer support, lead qualification and booking.
- Make content about each one with a "book a call" CTA. The leads that come in already have intent.
- **Follow-up: What if clients only want a one-time setup, not recurring payments?** Free or one-time is fine for getting testimonials, but clients must understand there's a **recurring API cost**. Over-deliver, get the testimonial, and move on if they don't want more work.
- Early on you're **demand-constrained**, so over-deliver to earn case studies. Once leads outpace what you can handle, you're **supply-constrained**: raise the barrier to entry (say from $2K to $5K) and hire.
- **Follow-up: Should I focus only on WhatsApp agents?** He's already sold 5–6 customer support builds at about $500 each. Yes, keep doubling down on WhatsApp customer support, because the process is proven and the case studies are relevant.

### 9. Finnian: How would you set up GitHub for a company AIOS? One repo per person plus a shared repo?
- **Give one person ownership of the global assets** (or one per team): the team wiki, team skills, writing rules and brand guidelines. Otherwise it gets messy.
- Nate's team is still figuring this out. Google is working on "OKF", an open knowledge format for syncing team knowledge.
- Humans have to keep shared data current. Otherwise the team's agents pull outdated or false information.
- Nate's team uses **Google Drive, split by department**. Each person can only access the folders they need. Leaders keep their areas updated, and every agent knows Drive is the team wiki.
- Each person builds their own local AIOS. This is a culture shift as much as a process change, and it gets harder as the company grows.
- **Follow-up: For a 12-person company that wants shared skills and agents, should they use Docs in Drive or a shared repo?** A **shared team repo**, for visibility. Package its contents as plugins that people install into Claude Code or Codex, so everyone stays up to date.
- You need an internal AI lead who owns the repo and can answer "what's GitHub?" questions.
- For non-technical staff, a plugin for the Claude desktop app is the easiest to install. GitHub is more tool-agnostic if the company later switches to Codex.
- **Follow-up: Can you create your own plugins?** Yes. It's like building a small MCP server or plugin, and Claude can help you build it and roll it out.

## Key takeaways
1. **Prove value with one agreed KPI.** An objective definition of done, defined out-of-scope items and a single business KPI protect your pricing and make testimonials and referrals easy.
2. **A demo isn't production.** Before scaling a support agent, test human edge cases, run evals and log every execution.
3. **Lead with outcomes, not "AI".** A tailored, outcome-driven portfolio on a website beats generic builds on GitHub.
4. **Start with your warm network.** Go from a free first project to a case study to retainers (3 × $10K = $30K/month solo). Fall back on Upwork and create content from day one.
5. **Follow your data.** Double down on what already works, like WhatsApp support agents, and raise prices once you're supply-constrained.
6. **Keep your AIOS accurate with pipelines and corrections.** Automate ingest and cleanup, and when it's wrong, have it explain where it looked and update CLAUDE.md. Sync code through GitHub and move `.env` with a password manager.
7. **Team setup:** one owner for shared knowledge, a shared repo or plugins for team skills, and a personal AIOS for each person. An AIOS moves easily between Claude Code and Codex.
8. **Formalize client deals** with communication deadlines, a ghosting clause and milestone payments. n8n is still fine for client work, but agentic tools are where things are heading.
