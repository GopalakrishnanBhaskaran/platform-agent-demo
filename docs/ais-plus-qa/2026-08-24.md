# AIS+ Q&A: Aug 24, 2026

**Host:** Nate Herk · **Length:** 62 min · **Source:** Fireflies

## Q&A

### 1. Wes: How do I structure a free build so it turns into a paid second project instead of anchoring me at zero?
- Context: a free inbox-routing build for a well-connected owner in a partner network. It's a handshake deal with no paperwork yet. His win condition is 99.9% correct forwarding.
- **Get a scope of work signed, even for a free handshake deal.** Without it, scope creep sets in ("could it also do this?").
- Agree on two things in writing:
  - Objective criteria you can prove. Not "the inbox agent is complete", but "all five forwarding rules are set up and routing correctly on these criteria."
  - A test dataset for the accuracy target, such as 100–500 emails labeled with where each should go. That's your source of truth for internal QA, prompt tuning and evaluation. Also check whether 99.9% is realistic.
- Draw the finish line: what the next 30–60 days deliver. Anything beyond that (extra features, a V2) becomes the second, paid project, ideally once you've earned trust.
- **Follow-up: How do I get from free to a price for the second project?** Use value-based pricing (Nate has a recent YouTube video on it). Use the scoping for project two to ask the questions that get you to that number.

### 2. Alan: What comes after "AI native" if I want to become a contract developer?
- Context: an experienced developer who already codes with AI and wants to help businesses in a niche become more efficient.
- First decide whether you want to own a business or work as a **forward deployed engineer**.
- Owning a business means a lot of sales and lead gen. Some people love that grind, and some would rather just build.
- If you're a builder:
  - Partner with someone who handles sales and ops while you own the tech.
  - Or take an internal AI consultant/FDE role. OpenAI, Microsoft and other large companies are hiring, at roughly $200K to $1M+ a year.
- Nate's AI certification program (first cohort, now in week two) aims to certify people for these roles or give agencies a credential that helps win bigger deals.
- Map your strengths, weaknesses and what you enjoy, then pick the path that fits. Watch Nate's video "So you've learned Claude, now what?"

### 3. Ryan: My first non-warm clients are excited. Should I ask for referrals now or wait until the build is done?
- Context: both clients came from a post in a Facebook business group offering free builds for case studies (one website, one automation). It's his first automation proof, and he has capacity for more.
- Nate checks two things first: whether you can take on 2–3 more projects, and how much proof you already have.
- **Wait until you've delivered undeniable value**, so the ask feels like a no-brainer. That could be the "wow" moment, or a month of results against the baseline metric.
- Referrers are risking their own relationships, so they'll only send people once they're confident in you.
- **Follow-up: How do I reach out to the referral once I get one?** Ask for an **introduction**, not contact details. The best case is an email thread like "I'd like you to meet Ryan, he just built this for me." Without an intro it's basically cold outreach again, so treat it that way.

### 4. Jeep: I've niched hard into owner-operated accounting firms (US and UK). Is that a strength or too narrow?
- Context: his angle is contrarian (he tells owners where AI would waste their money), and he uses the firms' hiring crisis as the wedge. He has two warm-network portfolio builds. Lead gen is hard because he isn't an accountant. He picked the niche for its heavy paperwork and data entry.
- Nate's first questions: how many clients have you had, and are you supply-constrained or demand-constrained?
- Never let the search for the perfect niche stop you from building and working with clients.
- **Write an avatar profile from your current clients** (age, location, challenges, business type). "I worked with two people just like you, and here's the value" beats a portfolio from another industry.
- Unless you're building a templated product or SaaS, keep winning work in the niche and move clients onto retainers.
- It's too early to worry about being pigeonholed, since many agencies will pivot with the trends.
- **Verdict: not too tight. It's an advantage.** Only go broader if you hit a real demand constraint and can't find leads that fit the avatar.

### 5. Ryan: What does a good AI-powered cold outreach system look like, and which tools are best?
- Context: a second Ryan, adding AI consulting and sales diagnostics to his fractional operator practice. He targets $10–100M manufacturers (CEOs, CFOs, owners), and a team already runs his cold email.
- **Three foundations:**
  - A proven offer with testimonials or proof.
  - Subject-matter expertise, including having done cold outreach successfully yourself.
  - A clearly defined avatar, so you can build a specific list (decision makers, high deliverability).
- Open with "here's what I did for someone else", plus a hard stat. "Can I send over this video?" tends to beat "Can I book a call?" as the call to action.
- Enrich leads after you've built a good list.
- **Tools:** Claude, Codex or any agent harness will do roughly the same job. Data is what matters.
  - Clay has strong data (waterfall enrichment), and Sales Navigator has its own advantages.
  - The best setups pull several data sources into one database.
  - Research where your avatar's data lives. For architects, for example, a niche industry database may beat Clay.
- **Follow-up: Should I lead with AI, or with an offer like a profit-leak map?**
  - Your offer is the product or service itself: a pain, a specific person, and a promise of how you solve it.
  - Enrich for context about the business and industry, find the pain point, and lead with it.
  - High volume gives a false sense of security. Analyze every batch (of 1,000 sent, how many clicked, where they dropped off). There are 11+ micro-decisions where people can drop out of the funnel.
- **Follow-up: Should I mention a paid diagnostic ($5–15K) in the outreach?** Nate's disclaimer is that he has never scaled a service business with cold outreach.
  - People want value for the least perceived effort, and leading with a diagnostic signals they'll have to get on a call.
  - A lead magnet they can benefit from even if they never reply is likely to convert better at the first step.
- **Follow-up: How did Nate scale?** Almost entirely through referrals and content.

### 6. Lucia: Will a SharePoint-based AIOS for an 11-person non-technical team break, and how do I get them to use it?
- Plan: Claude Teams and Cowork for everyone. One AIOS of markdown files maintained by the owner, shared through SharePoint via the Claude connector (not a local folder), with only the owner allowed to edit.
- **The main risk is human, not technical.**
- In each person's Cowork setup, add instructions (in CLAUDE.md, for example) to pull business context from that SharePoint folder, with simple routing rules. Users don't even need to know it's hitting SharePoint.
- **One editor is much easier.** The other 10 rely on one person to update, delete and keep the source of truth accurate. It works as long as that person stays consistent.
- Giving everyone edit access is a culture shift that needs a lot of trust, not just a new tool.
- Team agent builders and shared-knowledge tools (such as Buzz) are starting to appear, and Nate's team is looking at them too. Whatever the tech, human inconsistency remains a risk.

### 7. Kinto: Should I partner with my mentor, who brings clients, while I act as the operator who builds?
- Nate has been in partnerships that didn't work out, and negotiating the exit isn't fun.
- It's worth it if you trust the person, there's real upside, and they bring something you lack (capital, the idea, connections).
- **Structure it carefully from day one:**
  - One person has the final say, such as a 51/49 split.
  - The operating agreement spells out roles, responsibilities and the metrics each partner owns, which makes quarterly reviews and tough decisions easier.
  - Write it for the worst cases, not just the best case.
- An hour with a lawyer who sets up agreements like this is worth the money.
- Context: an early-stage AI agency market in Latin America. The mentor has the connections and handles sales. Kinto already builds automations for the mentor's business and teaches his team to create skills.
- Early on, a good mentor is the most valuable thing you can have. Set up the agreement well, then let the momentum carry you.
- **Follow-up: If he brings 10 clients, how do I avoid being stuck as the only builder?**
  - Nate's path: about 1.5 years as a solo freelancer (closing, scoping and building everything himself).
  - Then he joined an AI agency as a developer to learn how clients, PMs and salespeople work together.
  - Then he started his own agency as the salesperson and PM, no longer developing.
  - **Learn each role, hire for it, move up.** It's hard to hire for a role you've never done. Expect a few months to a few quarters at each step.

### 8. Alex: Unexpected costs (Clay testing, enrichment) came up on my free build. How do I raise them, and who covers what?
- Context: the client is a personal connection, and no scope of work is signed yet. Switching the architecture to use API calls and HTTP requests means Clay's $495/month plan, and the client wanted the cheapest options.
- **A free build should really be free.** A few hundred dollars, or even $1,000, is worth it for the case study, testimonial and likely referrals.
- **On paid builds, never bill testing separately. Build it into the price.**
  - Example: a $12K build over three months, paid as 3 × $4K. The stack is known (such as Clay and Claude) and the volume is estimated (5,000 messages/month), so estimate testing costs and add a couple of thousand to the price.
  - Asking for a paid API key right after signing feels bad to the client, so keep testing costs invisible.
  - Once the system is in production, switch to the client's own API keys, billed to their cards.
  - Take an upfront payment before development starts on paid deals.
- **Follow-up: Should I give two months of free maintenance before a retainer?**
  - It depends on the expected load. 10 hours a week is a lot, and Nate would try to charge even for about 3 hours a week.
  - One free month to iron out issues and meet the promised scope is reasonable.
  - Long-term free maintenance only makes sense as part of a paid engagement, for example if the client hires you for another project.

### 9. Ryan: In which industries does opening outreach with "AI" work, and in which does it hurt?
- Nate can't name specific industries. His clients came through his videos or referrals, so they were already warm.
- You'd know from inside an industry, for example where AI has a negative connotation or people are so far behind that the word scares them.
- A business still running on paper forms and filing cabinets probably won't care about "AI" at all.
- Industries under heavy pressure and looking for relief may respond a bit better to an "AI solution" framing.
- **Golden rule:** lead with a specific outcome they care about that you've already proven for someone else. Show the value before you mention AI at all.

### 10. Wes: What kills members who fail to go from first client to a real business, and what do the ones who make it do?
- **Before the first client:** the wrong mindset. Chase your first 10 no's, not your first yes. Don't take rejection personally, because "volume negates luck" (Alex Hormozi).
- **Pricing:** be ready for "tell me exactly how you got there." If you can justify the number, state it and stay silent. It's still value-based pricing, not a scam.
- **Scaling too fast without codified processes:**
  - Sales hires overpromise, the CRM goes stale, handoffs get mixed up, and engineers get PRDs without enough detail.
  - Every person you add multiplies the connections and the risk (2 people have 1 connection, 3 people have 3, and so on).
- Nate's "hell month" (March 2025): 11 sales calls, 11 yeses, all signed. They then had to scramble to hire, missed deadlines and had underpriced the work.

### 11. Jeep: For an accounting client's data-entry workflow, should Claude shape the proposal, and should I build a front end?
- Context: a self-hosted workflow. Staff drop bank statements and invoices into a folder, and AI extracts the data into Excel.
- **Proposal: do both.** Use Claude as a devil's advocate, ask Codex too, and compare notes. Your own judgment and taste decide.
- The only harm is blindly passing an AI's answer to the client.
- **Front end: no.** It just adds touch points and things for them to remember.
- The best automations are ones the client doesn't notice running. Make the folder they already use the trigger, and change their day-to-day as little as possible.
- **Follow-up: What if they drop the wrong files in the folder and break it?**
  - That's a human edge case. Tell them which file types belong there, and add filters to the workflow.
  - Test edge cases, and monitor the workflow in production, because humans are unpredictable.

### 12. Lucia: How do I price building a custom MCP for a niche client tool with no native connector?
- Example: a gym management tool like Gym Master that has no native Claude connector.
- Nate's team has never built a fully custom API or MCP. They usually look at browser use, computer use or a simple macro first.
- **Definitely price it in.** It's part of the solution, it's valuable IP for the client, and it unlocks more automation for them.
- With value-based pricing, recognize that none of the rest would work as smoothly without it. Weight it in, but not so heavily that the client loses interest.
- Estimate the effort and time involved to protect your margins.
- **Follow-up: Isn't it easier to have Claude in Chrome pull the data, or just download and re-upload it?**
  - Yes, that's a quicker alternative.
  - If the task needs no AI or has little variability, a macro that clicks the same spots in the same order is often enough to download a report or enter data.

### 13. Reginald: When a client balks at your price, do you hold firm or negotiate?
- **Don't lower the price without lowering the scope.** Discounting teaches the client this is a negotiation, and you're a consultant, not a negotiator.
- Example: you quote $15K and they're only comfortable with $7K. Take it back to your team, cut the scope, and return with a ~$7K version.
- Clients are usually receptive, and the smaller project ships faster.
- Once the system pays for itself (ideally in about 3 months), they've felt the ROI in their own business and are far more willing to raise the budget.

## Key takeaways
1. **Scope free builds tightly.** Use a signed scope of work, provable criteria and a client test dataset. Anything beyond that is the paid second project, priced on value.
2. **Earn referrals before asking for them.** Deliver undeniable value first, then ask for an introduction rather than contact details.
3. **A tight niche is an advantage early on.** Build an avatar from current clients, and broaden only if demand dries up.
4. **Cold outreach runs on data and offer, not the AI tool.** You need a proven offer, a specific list, enriched pain-point messaging, a low-friction call to action or lead magnet, and analysis of every batch.
5. **Pricing discipline:** absorb testing costs on free builds and build them into paid quotes. Never cut price without cutting scope, and be ready to justify your number.
6. **Scale deliberately.** Learn each role before hiring for it and codify your processes. Signing 11 clients in a month broke delivery. Partnerships need one final decision-maker and a written agreement.
7. **Design for humans.** Keep one editor for a team AIOS, change users' daily routine as little as possible, and add guardrails for edge cases.
8. **Custom connectors are billable IP.** Price MCP builds into the project, and consider macros or browser automation when the task is low-variability.
