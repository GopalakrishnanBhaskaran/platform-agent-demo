### 3. Adam: Setting up Cowork for a school with decades of Google Drive files. What folder structure, how much to ingest, and should I use the wiki?
- **There's no "correct" structure that Claude expects.** Knowing that takes the pressure off. Two tests:
  1. Is it intuitive to *you*? Could you drill down and find things by hand?
  2. Does the agent understand it? That comes down to routing in `CLAUDE.md` and the context files.
- Brainstorm the structure with the agent. Make the top-level folders the "balls you juggle" in that project.
- **Nate's example:**
  - His most-used folder is `Projects/`. He's switching from organising it by month to by quarter.
  - He sometimes opens a project in Claude Code on its own, but likes his main operating system to have visibility into all of them.
- **For a large knowledge base, reverse-engineer from the questions:**
  - What triggers someone to search the files, and what answer do they need back?
  - Fetching a specific item (a book from a library) is a different design from getting an answer out of many items (what is mitochondria?). Sometimes you need both.
  - Start small, scale up, and collect example questions if you can.
- For Adam's case ("what have parents complained about most over the decades?"), semantic search probably fits.
- **The wiki** is great for finding relations between things within one domain. It doesn't scale like a database, though. At that size a formal RAG or graph RAG may fit better.
- **Pre-process before ingesting.** Decide what in a PDF matters: just the text, or also styling and charts (which means OCR). If you only need the text, convert to markdown. It's cheaper, faster and easier for agents to read.

### 5. Alex: What's one thing you wish you'd learned earlier about running an automation business?
- **Scoping and discovery caused his first five or so failures.**
  - The worst: a RAG system to match incoming proposals with past quotes. He'd built it before, so he scoped it lightly.
  - The data turned out to be hundreds of thousands of messy records, not a few hundred. He undercharged, couldn't make it work, refunded in full and protected the brand.
- **Scope properly:**
  - Walk the process front to back with the subject-matter expert who actually does it, not just the buyer.
  - Cover everything that can go wrong and how edge cases are handled today.
  - Ask about the data: what comes in, where it's written, and how much there is.
  - Asking "too many" questions is exactly right. Early scoping can be free; once you have authority, charge for it.
- **If starting an agency today, he'd start with education, not projects:**
  - A $50–150 one-hour session teaching Claude Code, where you build the client's first skill, is a much easier ask than a multi-thousand-dollar project.
  - It builds trust, so the later project scope is a warm close.

### 6. Matt: A $40M/year business has weak LinkedIn, TikTok and YouTube. Any Claude Code skills or tools (Blotato) for lightweight marketing?
- **No specific skill.** It depends on their data. Study any past posts that outperformed and build a skill around why.
- **Be clear on the avatar.** The goal is usually revenue and customers, not views. A mix such as 1 in 4 posts broad (for reach) and 3 in 4 aimed at conversion can work.
- **Content automation needs content expertise.** Use skills that carry a subject-matter expert's knowledge (Nate's example: a YouTube expert's hook-to-end skill).
- **Find out what they want:** an automation that runs for them, or to learn the tool? Those are very different engagements.
- **Blotato** is a good fit for scheduling and posting across platforms, and for ideation.
- **Prove before you automate.** They're asking for a content program and its automation at once. Prove the concept first.
- **Look for the real constraint.** Most requested automations (70%+) are front-end marketing. The bigger profit wins are often "clogs and holes" in the middle of the business. If you promise impressions you can't deliver, you'll take the blame.

### 7. Reeves: My wiki gets less focused as it grows (300–500 docs from database books). Can I layer something on top, or should I move to RAG or graph RAG?
- **The wiki doesn't scale like a vector database.** Nate's main wiki is about 60–70 markdown files and works fine. If you add to it daily, it will lose effectiveness at some point.
- When he hits that point, he'll split it into buckets, or move to graph RAG or plain RAG.
- **Progressive disclosure for markdown:**
  - Skills use YAML front matter to say what each file covers. Copy that idea with a master routing document: "for these concepts, read these six files."
  - It scales well because the agent is routed to the right spot before it reads anything. Karpathy's wiki is relational and doesn't route this way.

### 9. Neil: Exec-admin agents on Paperclip burn through Haiku limits. Should I move to an open model, or try another approach, on almost no budget?
- **Context:** non-technical users whose interface is email. They send a request, the agent completes it and replies, day or night.
- **The open-model trade-off:** somewhat worse and slower, which may not matter here. Run the biggest model you can. Today's open models match Sonnet 3.7, which is enough for general assistant work but weaker at deep reasoning.
- **Guardrails:**
  - Give each agent its own email account and logins.
  - Give it no permission to delete or do anything else you don't want.
  - Expose it to the real environment to learn how it fails.
- **Consider Hermes instead of Paperclip.** It builds skills, feels always-on, and can check email on a heartbeat (every 30 minutes, say). An open model with Hermes is worth testing.
- Build one agent, iterate for a few weeks, then scale.

### 10. Mike: How do I add automations to my coaching business for home-service pros (roofers, landscapers), who aren't open to tech?
- **Best:** a case study from someone in a similar position, showing the money or time gained. **Second best:** a demo.
- People have to meet you halfway ("you can't sell water to someone who isn't thirsty"). A free build can open their eyes, but it carries the risk they still don't buy.
- **Use hard numbers.** You know their process, so do the math: "this saves 5 hours a week and $10K a month." Objective ROI is hard to argue with.
- Leave a "no" on good terms. They'll think of you when they're ready.
- **Claude Code or n8n?**
  - The end automation is similar either way. Nate would use Claude Code with Codex.
  - Ask what the client wants in hand. If they want to see and own the workflow, n8n's visual interface is still what many businesses prefer.

### 12. Liam: Are voice agents useful for outbound lead generation for a high-end renovation company?

## Key takeaways
1. **Folder structure has one test:** intuitive to you, and routed for the agent through `CLAUDE.md`. Organise around the "balls you juggle".
2. **Design knowledge bases from the questions they must answer.** Item lookup and answer synthesis need different designs. Convert to markdown before ingesting.
3. **LLM wikis work within one domain** (Nate's is about 60–70 files) but don't scale like a vector database. Split into buckets, add a routing index, or move to RAG or graph RAG.
4. **Use Claude Code and Codex together:** Claude for creativity and planning, Codex for detail, debugging and code review.
5. **Scoping is where projects fail.** Talk to the real process owner, cover the edge cases, and size the data.
6. **Start with paid education** to build trust before pitching projects.
7. **Find the real constraint** before automating marketing, and prove a process before automating it.
8. **Cold outbound voice or SMS carries real legal risk.** Use opted-in contacts only.
