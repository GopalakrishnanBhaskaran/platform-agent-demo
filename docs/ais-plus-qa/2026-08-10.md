## Q&A

### 1. Stuart: You say skip n8n and go straight to Claude Code. What's my path as a beginner building an agency?
- With clients you sell the outcome, not the tool. Some Upwork posts do still ask for an n8n or Claude workflow by name.
- Nate skips n8n because he gets far more done, much faster, with Claude Code.
- n8n just added a Claude-style agent builder. Nate hasn't tested it, but it's worth a look if you already use n8n. Claude and Codex can also plug into n8n.
- n8n taught Nate production basics (webhooks, API keys, wiring up HTTP requests and evals yourself). You don't need it to deliver value, but Claude Code won't force you to learn those things.
- **Risk: a false sense of security.** A common pattern: a build ships, breaks in production, the client feels scammed, and the agency's reputation takes the hit.
- Learn the difference between a workflow that works and one that works in production: it has been through evals and fails safely (doesn't delete a database or send the wrong thing to a client).
- Recommendation: jump into Claude Code, but don't get attached to the name. Codex does the same job, and harnesses (such as Hermes agent) and models will keep coming and going.
- Build your skills, AIOS and automations to be tool-agnostic. Evergreen principles are what let consultants ship automations that don't break.

### 2. Alex: Should client accounts and infrastructure be under the client's ownership from day one, or moved over once the system is proven?
- Context: Clay accounts, domains, inboxes, API accounts and other infrastructure.
- Nate's agency almost always had the client set everything up from day one and built inside the client's environment.
- That makes the switch-over easy. One late handoff took about 4 hours of copying credentials, moving things and retesting.
- Frame it as your method: on the kickoff call, walk them through creating the accounts and granting access. They also get visibility into the work.
- A few clients push back, and you can adapt. Most don't know what they need, though, and accept the setup when you lead.

### 3. Diogo: How do I pitch AI automation work at my company when I'm not yet confident as a builder?
- Context: Diogo isn't a coder but has built apps and simple automations for himself, and is now the "AI guy" at work. He landed it by showing those builds. He plans a two-step proposal: first map processes and problems, then suggest tools and automations.
- You're in a good spot. The goal is to avoid overpromising and under-delivering.
- **Use the community:** scope the project first (opportunities, price point, what you can pay), then post it. You'll likely find technical collaborators, and some may help for free to gain experience.
- Avoid imposter syndrome by being honest about what you can do.
- Suggested pitch: an hour-long session to examine the business and its processes. Your strength is spotting automation and AI opportunities, and you can bring in a more experienced developer to build.
- Either way, hand over a useful deliverable (where the inefficiencies are), keep learning and stay in touch. That protects your reputation.

### 4. Ed: How do I build a people-profiling model with subject expertise but limited technical skills?
- Context: inputs are things like a person's writing, observations or a LinkedIn profile. The output is a profile of patterns and motivations that predicts behavior (or supports self-understanding), drawing on hundreds of patterns such as a confidence vs competence mismatch.
- Nate's read: this is a few robust prompts and generative work, not an API-heavy automation. Not very technical, which is good news.
- It's fairly low risk, especially with a human-in-the-loop approval before any output is given.
- **Steps:**
  - Start a new Claude project (or any tool) and add the **Grill Me skill** (Nate shared his video link; the skill is in the community).
  - Ask Claude to use it to pull out everything you know about modeling. Keep answering until it feels complete.
  - Collect as many golden examples (inputs plus outputs you trust) as you can and give them to Claude as the standard.
  - Walk it through inputs, the decisions to make and what to look at. Review the output, say what was good or bad and why, and repeat until it matches the golden set.
- **Make it prove it learned:** after each correction, ask it to show where it updated the prompt or saved the mistake. Otherwise it may forget by the next session.

### 5. Vitas: Where should a small team's markdown "central brain" live, and how should the team use it?
- Context: an insurance agency with a team of 11, using Claude and VS Code.
- Markdown works well as knowledge (see Nate's LLM wiki videos and context files). The hard part is keeping everyone in sync.
- Nate's team is building a back-end script that syncs everyone's markdown files. It's finicky (files locked while open, odd change logs). Automatic sync is hard unless the work happens on a cloud platform.
- **Option 1:** agents connect to a few Google Docs as an always-synced brain that everyone's agents can read and update at once, with role-based access (view vs edit). Good for docs that change a lot, but agents make API calls each time.
- **Option 2:** static markdown files or PDFs in Google Drive or a GitHub repo. A human pushes each update and deletes the old version. The risk is people forgetting.
- **It's a people problem more than a tech problem.** Agree as a team on how docs get updated and deleted, so nobody works off stale data or keeps fresh data to themselves. Notion, Google Docs or similar all work.
- Nate's team is actively working on this and will share what they learn.

### 6. Waseem: What's more reliable than vision-based control for legacy desktop apps with no API?
- Context: Waseem reads the accessibility tree, and when it has no IDs or names he falls back to screenshots plus AI picking coordinates.
- With no programmatic access, Nate defaults to computer use or browser use. **Codex** is the best he's used.
- He's also had success with GPT 5.6 SOL plus the Playwright CLI or similar CLIs. Claude Code is decent but behind Codex. He wouldn't recommend Firecrawl's computer use.
- Headless browser sessions with persistent cookies can keep logins saved.
- Watch for anti-bot detection: very fast, bot-like clicking can get you blocked.
- **Follow-up: Can computer use be trained on specific tasks?** Yes. It works from vision, and you can give it a screen recording of you clicking while you explain the steps. Other computer-use agents can also pick up the skills he's already built.
- **Follow-up: I build on a Mac, but the pharmacy systems run on Windows. Do I have to test on a PC?** In Nate's experience computer use doesn't change much across operating systems unless the tool is OS-specific. He runs the same skills on a MacBook and a Windows PC.
- **Follow-up: I custom-coded it to keep patient data (PHI) local and avoid extra business associate agreements. Is there a better way?** If the steps are always the same and need no reasoning, skip AI.
  - Use a deterministic **macro** that clicks set pixel coordinates in order, with copy and paste in between. Ask Claude Code to build it.
  - It runs faster and helps privacy, since no AI sees the data.
  - Because it's pixel-based, build and test it on the actual device it will run on.

### 7. Shilpi: When should the evaluation framework be defined, during design or during the build?
- **Up front, during scoping and before you accept payment.** Know the pain point, the proposed solution, the inputs and outputs, and where the data transformation happens.
- Before signing the scope of work, confirm the client has a golden (evaluation) dataset. For an email agent, that might be 100 examples of good replies to specific support queries.
- Avoid reaching QA with the build almost done, only to find the client has no data. If they can't even define it, you may not be building the right solution.
- **Follow-up: I already built a tool with Claude and skipped evals. How do I evaluate it now?**
  - Context: a pre-screening tool for land eligibility in US federal conservation programs. It uses no LLM, just a known formula (rainfall, crop history, soil erodibility, elevation) with public data APIs such as NOAA. She knows the formula but has no set of correct outputs.
  - Get correct answers first, for example by running a batch manually.
  - Ask Claude to break it: edge cases and holes. If you built it with Claude, have Codex do the same. In Nate's latest video, Codex clicked around a SaaS and tried odd inputs, which surfaced many fixes.
  - Outputs that are objectively right or wrong are easier to evaluate.
  - Expect a V2 and V3, because the real world exposes things testing won't.
  - For production: log every run, send error notifications, and give users a way to flag a wrong result so you can trace that run. Aim for visibility and safe failures.
  - Nate has almost never shipped an AI system he didn't have to fix later.

### 9. Hector: Should I use my 10K-subscriber newsletter or my 27K-follower Instagram brand to get agency clients?
- Think **pain, promise, person** for each of the agency, newsletter and personal brand: the pain, how your product answers it, and who is on the other side of the screen.
- If all three line up, you get a flywheel: free value in the newsletter, upsells into the agency, and the same with content on your personal brand.
- If they differ (say, a fitness newsletter and an ads agency), the newsletter will be hard to turn into clients.
- Nate's funnel: YouTube (free value) → free community (email capture) → newsletter (warm nurture, discounts, tripwire offers) → Plus group → certification and events.
- People who never pay still get value and like the brand. For those who will pay, show them the path.
- Once or twice a month, send a case study in the newsletter ("here's what we built for a client; book a call") and share the same stories on your personal brand.
- **Follow-up:** Hector realized his audience is mostly AI enthusiasts rather than business owners. Nate said an unclear ICP is normal in AI. His own YouTube has no single avatar, while an agency's ICP is clearer and gets clearer once you niche down.

### 10. Daryl: How do I make a Retell voice agent for a roofing client sound as human as possible, and is Retell a good platform for it?
- Yes, Retell is a really good platform.
- Nate always had voice agents introduce themselves as AI. It's optional, but it protects the brand, since they can't fully pass as human yet (awkward pauses, talking over people, weak intonation).
- Avoid awkward silence. When it's looking something up, have it say "just give me a second". Small pauses help.
- Try different voice models in Retell, or bring in an ElevenLabs voice you like or a cloned voice. A good voice matters a lot, and so does the prompt.
- Some voices breathe. You can add background sound (dishes and silverware for a restaurant hostess agent) so it feels less perfect.
- **The real answer: call it a ton.** Note what felt off, re-prompt, and call again. Nate's team spent hours and hours talking to agents.

### 11. Anon: Should a portfolio have 2–3 polished apps or many small experiments, and what documentation shows compliance and safety?
- Show whatever is closest to the prospect's pain. Thirty builds don't matter unless one solves their problem.
- "You're losing leads because of X, and here's a build that solved X" beats a big portfolio. Pick the piece that fits their situation.
- **Regulated industries:** address the regulations, where the data lives (local), encryption, and how PII is anonymized.
- **Other businesses** fear horror stories (agents deleting databases, sending the wrong info to clients). Show your phased rollout, months of evaluation before production, error notifications and logging, and human-in-the-loop approval.
- The point is to show the system was designed with thought, not built from a few prompts after a YouTube tutorial.

### 16. Kira: Are there pitfalls in pointing Codex to my CLAUDE.md files instead of duplicating them?
- Context: instead of an AGENTS.md and skills that mirror her Claude files, Kira added pointers to her CLAUDE.md and skills. She also renamed project-level CLAUDE.md files to PROJECT.md to be more agnostic.
- Pointers are fine as long as both agents pick them up and the routing is correct.
- Claude may not read PROJECT.md natively unless CLAUDE.md tells it to look for one in each project folder (she had done this).
- The only cost is slightly lower token efficiency (a couple hundred extra tokens), which isn't a big deal. Scaling to hundreds or thousands of PROJECT.md files could cause issues.
- **Follow-up: How do I route Codex at the project level without duplicating files?** Put an AGENTS.md in each project that just references the CLAUDE.md, so you only ever update one file. There's no wrong way.

### 17. Alex: Which metrics should a wholesale lead gen pilot track to prove business value?
- Context: options include cost per qualified lead, reply rate, meetings, new accounts and revenue attributed.
- A good automation will probably move a few of these, and that's fine.
- Present the options with baselines and ask the stakeholder which one matters most to the bottom line. They'll tell you, and that's the one to move.
- The goal is a client so thrilled they want to give you more projects.

### 18. Emmanuel: How do I build an agency when consistent clients are hard to find?
- Context: six website projects and two automation workflows closed for local businesses so far.
- **Lever 1: past clients.** More work from an existing client is easier than a new one. Go back to all eight and see what else you can do.
- **Lever 2: warm outreach.** Contact anyone who would recognize your name and ask if they know someone who wants AI automation help. Lead with your case studies and proof.
- **Lever 3: Upwork bids** once warm outreach is exhausted. It's not easy, but it beats cold outreach: buyers are interested, have budget and often want to move fast.
- The goal is retainers. Position yourself as a consultant by proving it: diagnose the constraints, reframe them, find the opportunities, build, and move the metrics.
- **Prove the ROI.** Many people build a great automation but never show the client it's great. "You paid me $600 and I made your business $7,000" is what makes clients want to keep spending.

## Key takeaways
1. **Learn on Claude Code or Codex, but stay tool-agnostic.** n8n is optional. Production habits (evals, safe failures) are what keep builds from breaking.
2. **Define evals before you sign.** Confirm a golden dataset, then stress-test with Claude and Codex, log every run and add error alerts.
3. **Have clients own their accounts from day one** to avoid painful handoffs.
4. **Be honest about your skill level.** Sell the opportunity-finding audit and bring in community developers to build.
5. **Use deterministic macros for fixed click paths.** Save computer use (Codex is best) for tasks that need vision or reasoning.
6. **Shared team knowledge is a people problem.** Choose always-synced docs or disciplined static files, and agree on update rules.
7. **Sell to the client's pain.** Pick portfolio pieces, metrics and offers (audit vs concrete build) by what the client cares about, and align pain, promise and person across channels.
8. **For consistent clients,** go to past clients, then warm outreach, then Upwork. Prove ROI to win retainers.
