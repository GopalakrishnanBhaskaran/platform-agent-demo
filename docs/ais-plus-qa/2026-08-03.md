### 1. Monty: How should I use a strong case study in warm outreach DMs?
- Use the proof in your outreach, but keep building your list and stick to a steady daily cadence of how many people you reach out to.
- **Warm outreach is about starting a conversation, not selling.** The goal is to find people who have the need or interest. That's where the case study comes in.
- Go after lookalikes. If your case study is with a plumber, get in front of as many plumbers as possible, because you've already solved their niche's pain point.

### 2. Christine: What were your three biggest takeaways from the conference?
- **1. Start taking action.** Most people Nate met were doing far more than they thought and were waiting for validation, which often comes down to imposter syndrome.
  - Lower the barrier to entry. Early on you're optimizing for skills and learning, not money. The business comes downstream.
  - Instead of racing to your first yes, race to your **first 10 nos**. You stop feeling like a failure after 20 unanswered messages, and you learn which messages land and how to run a first prospect call.
- **2. Unclear offer and avatar stop people from making content.** Many in the room wanted to post but hadn't.
  - Pick one specific real person and pretend you're talking to them on camera. Nate started by imagining his manager at Goldman, where he wanted to be the team's AI person. It made both his style and his audience clearer.
- **3. We're much earlier than we think.** The AI news cycle is stressful, but don't compare yourself to people who already have five clients. Network with them and learn from them instead. You're probably the most AI-knowledgeable person in your own circle.
- **Follow-up: Christine has a healthcare background but is worried about healthcare rules. Should she avoid the niche?** Nate's gut says stick with it.
  - Short term it's slower and can feel frustrating while others take off in other niches.
  - In a year, though, she'd have a dialed-in offer in a space many people deliberately avoid. She already speaks the language and has a passion for it.
  - If she needs clients now, there's nothing wrong with starting more general first.

### 3. John: What QA and error-proofing prompts should I use while building a CRM and email automation engine for a private equity firm?
- Use the same idea as the STORM method (which John already uses for planning) for stress testing, but with personas such as a front-end dev, back-end dev, stakeholder and project manager. Have them poke holes in the plan and throw edge cases at it.
- You can also download existing development or QA skills.
- Have a different harness and model review it too. Nate has **Codex run adversarial reviews**, especially on products Claude built.
- You won't catch every edge case up front. Some will show up in production, and that's okay.
- **Internal QA is about evals, not prompts.** Get a **golden dataset** from the client: inputs where you already know the correct answer. Run them through the system, measure accuracy, fix what's wrong and re-run until it meets the criteria for production.
- **Follow-up: Why Codex instead of a Claude agent as reviewer?** Codex's model and harness are fundamentally different, with a different toolset and a different way of looping. Roughly 10% of the result is the model and 90% is the harness. Almost every Codex review Nate runs flags at least one thing.
- **Follow-up: Codex plugin in Claude Code, or open the project in Codex?** The plugin works fine for a review only. Nate prefers to open a separate terminal running Codex on the project folder so it can also fix bugs.
- **Follow-up: What does Codex need from the project?** The Claude-specific parts are `CLAUDE.md` and the `.claude` folder (skills, agents, settings). Codex uses `AGENTS.md`, which is the same as `CLAUDE.md` so you can copy it, plus `.codex` and `.agents` folders. Easiest route: ask Claude Code to research how Codex works and create whatever Codex needs for that folder.
- **Follow-up: How often should I run these reviews?** It depends on complexity. Run them after major milestones or before you start running evals.

### 6. Ed: What are the key skills for being "the AI person"?
- Nate has a YouTube video on six AI skills to learn now that covers this, and he dropped it in the chat.
- **The big one is verification:** getting AI to check and improve its own output before you see it.
- Think of an intern building a PDF deck. You'd check spacing and fact-check sources. AI can do those checks itself first.
- Instead of a 60–70% first draft that needs rounds of feedback, verification loops can get you to 80–90% right away.
- How to do it: give the task, describe what good looks like, and tell it to keep checking until it meets your definition of good before answering.
- You should still review what AI gives you. But models are now much better at verification, so skipping it means using them inefficiently.

### 7. Madi: How do I mention my case study in a DM without trying too hard, and offer "pay if you love it" instead of free work?
- Context: Madi has a case study but no paying client yet.
- "Pay if you love it" works because it reduces the client's risk.
- **Make it objective by agreeing on an outcome** instead of "love".
- Example: a sales automation for booked calls. If the baseline is 5 booked calls a week, the client only pays once you move it to 10 a week, say within the first 3 months.
- An agreed outcome protects you from a client who simply says they don't love it. It also plants the seed of what's possible, since doubling booked calls is something most owners want.

### 10. Hector: How do I turn my ~23K Instagram following into clients? Or should I still focus on Upwork?

### 13. Valentina: What case studies should I show a local consignment shop that doesn't seem interested in operations or content automations?
- From Nate's conference talk: **businesses only grow when you remove constraints.**
- No case study will resonate unless it addresses their actual constraint, on either the supply side or the demand side. Talk to them first to find the bottleneck.
- Most owners think they need more leads, but many couldn't fulfil more leads anyway.
- Example: one owner asked for lead generation, but leads weren't showing up to appointments and paid customers weren't booking a second visit. Nate built a follow-up and reactivation system instead, which grew her business more than lead gen would have.

### 14. Mohammed: How do I estimate the monthly cost of running AI workflows for my first client, a recruiting agency?
- Exact numbers are hard, so **err on the high side.**
- Estimate throughput × tokens. Example: a system replying to about 20 emails a week at roughly 20,000 tokens each (a guess), which Nate's example turned into about $300/month in API billing.
- Explain that it's a **variable cost, not fixed**. As the business grows, usage grows, so expect a roughly linear rise in API billing over the year. Frame it as more usage meaning more time saved.
- **Have the client pay API and third-party costs directly.** Passing them through on your invoice is messy, adds follow-up, gives the client less visibility and can create trust problems.
- **Follow-up: The client has a list of about 10 automations it wants to make AI-driven and hands-off. Should I dig in before quoting?** Yes.
  - Don't take on 10 automations at once. Work one at a time; Nate uses **30-day milestones**.
  - Your job is to work out which ones are actually best for the business.
  - A client who insists on all 10 or will go elsewhere is racing to the bottom on price. Whether to take that on depends on your situation, but it's usually a red flag for a long-term client.

## Key takeaways
1. **Optimize early for reps, not revenue.** Race to your first 10 nos, and remember you're earlier and further ahead than you think.
2. **Clarity on offer and avatar drives content.** Talk to one specific person, make sure your audience matches your ICP, and always give a call to action.
3. **Sell to the real constraint.** Use case studies to target lookalike businesses, and diagnose the bottleneck (often follow-up, not leads) before choosing what to show.
4. **Reduce client risk with outcomes.** Tie payment to moving a baseline (for example, 5 → 10 booked calls a week). For an untested product, a free or outcome-based pilot is worth more than a small fee.
5. **Price on value, not hours.** Your first price sets the relationship: a few clients on big retainers, or a productized offer for many.
6. **QA with personas, a second harness and evals.** Stress test with role personas, run Codex adversarial reviews and use a golden dataset before production. Build verification loops into everyday AI work.
7. **Be upfront about running costs.** Estimate throughput × tokens, err high, have clients pay API costs directly, and deliver one automation at a time in 30-day milestones.
8. **Don't build a business on a feature platforms will absorb.** Fold it into a broader offer or use it as a lead magnet.
