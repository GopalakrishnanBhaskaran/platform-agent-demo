### 1. Christine: What are you getting Fable to work on over the next two days?
- Nate has been studying what makes Fable so good beyond the model itself: the instructions it follows and how it delegates work.
- Watch your limits. A `/goal` on max reasoning can burn through a whole session limit in about an hour.
- **High effort is the most balanced.** xhigh and max tend to overthink. They sometimes give worse results and spin up extra workflows that eat your limit.
- **Use Fable as an orchestrator,** like a high-level manager. It sets the strategy, helps make the plan and decides who to delegate to, rather than doing everything itself.
- The aim is to use the least intelligent model that still gets the job done. Give Fable routing rules so it spins up Sonnet 5 or Opus workers, or hands complex tasks to Codex.
- **What he uses it for:** mostly knowledge work, such as research, planning, video editing and LLM wikis that give Fable as much knowledge as possible. He does little hardcore coding, though Fable shines there too.

- "Can AI do this?" is a spectrum, not yes/no. If Opus handles ~60% of a task, he pushes to see whether Fable can do 80–90%.
- **Skills audit:** he had Fable review all his projects, CLAUDE.md files and skills. The prompt (from a tweet) told it to act as a senior engineer writing handoff docs for a new team of junior engineers. It improved everything, flagged gaps and created a few new skills.

- He uses Codex as a reviewer and second opinion. His view is that Codex has better intelligence than many Claude models but worse taste, so using both balances out.

### 2. Brian: How will the AI consulting space evolve: consolidation, roll-ups or expansion?
- Context: Brian runs a cybersecurity firm that advises companies on implementing AI securely (strategy, not the build). In past tech waves he's seen many solo operators start up, then most drop out or get consolidated.
- **Security gap:** cyber insurance quotes Nate has seen exclude AI-related attacks because they're so unpredictable. Brian expects coverage to come, with guardrails.
- **Nate's pivot:** under 2% of people want to be entrepreneurs. Most want to get hired, get promoted and not be replaced by AI, so Nate's channel and company are shifting their messaging.
- A **certification program launches in August** for both external consultants and people seeking jobs or promotions.
- Businesses feel board and competitor pressure to have an AI strategy, and they're spending without seeing ROI. Whether they hire an agency, a big firm like McKinsey or someone in-house, the core problems are **governance and evals** (the two biggest of ~7–8 pillars), plus knowing whether someone can actually do the job.
- Anthropic and OpenAI moving into consulting (with Goldman and Blackstone) suggests the models alone aren't the moat, as Chinese and open-source models keep improving.
- Nate imagines companies running their own models in 5–10 years, for data, control, cost and privacy. Forward-deployed engineers give the labs a second source of stickiness and revenue.
- **Follow-up: If models commoditize and the harness is the moat, where is the value going, and how should a security firm pick builders to partner with?**
  - Companies will bring AI in-house, but that shouldn't scare agencies. **Find a niche.** Your moat is the data and experience of seeing the same processes again and again, spotting the holes and building a framework to plug them. Then go upmarket.
  - Big firms charge hundreds of thousands of dollars just for an audit, so there's a big opportunity below them.
  - Marketing analogy: every company now has marketing in-house but still hires media buyers and platform agencies. Agencies that don't niche down in the next 5–10 years will find it much harder.
- **Two open problems Nate is watching:**
  - More agents than humans now browse the web. An agent scans thousands of results and returns ~5, so your positioning and avatar must be very specific (people are calling this AEO, among other names).
  - Content is now easy to make. When he started, about 6 people made n8n videos. The challenge is reaching high-intent people who will pay, not just getting views.
- Brian added that even large organizations outsource specialty and 24/7 security work. Most CISOs lack AI-risk skills, and there's little good training material.
- **Partnering opportunity:** security teams can add a lot of value alongside automation agencies, especially for local and on-prem setups. Nate plans more local and open-source content on YouTube in the back half of 2026.
- Brian's point on resilience: the Fable outage showed the risk of depending on one model. Plan for model routing and redundancy. His team is training small in-house models on open-source bases for single tasks. Redundancy, security and cost will matter more and more.

### 4. Josue: As a beginner, should I learn the fundamentals, building, sales or outreach first?
- Context: Josue's goal is to land a first client and go full-time. He has only used ChatGPT for chats and has no technical background. You don't need one.
- **Two phases:** learn and build first, then the business side.
- Build with intention: make things you'd use every day or week, even personal ones (e.g., a gym schedule or macro tracker). You get real feedback, iterate and feel the ROI, which makes you want to build more.
- It also builds client trust. Showing tools you use daily ("eat your own dog food") proves you, not just your skills.
- Start with the AIS+ course or Nate's 10-hour YouTube course. Jot down repetitive or time-consuming tasks as you go. Then open Claude Code and say "here's something I do every week."
- **Most important: explain every step and decision clearly** so the system can replicate it. Second is your judgment, taste and feedback across many iterations.
- Move to the business side once you have 3–4 agents you use all the time.
- **Proof:** the best path is making content while you learn, as Nate did. Otherwise build a portfolio, such as a website or a big PDF showing what you built, how it works and the value.
- **Outreach order:** content, then warm outreach, then cold (email, LinkedIn). A warm lead is anyone who knows your name. Ask "this is what I'm learning, do you know anyone who'd be interested?", not "will you buy?"
- **Mindset:** it takes 10× more outreach than you think. Optimize for reps, not money, and aim to get your first **10 nos** fast. Write down why each no happened and fix it next time.

### 7. Anon: Should I build one AIOS for my marketing business or a separate one for each client?
- Context: the asker's wedding videography and photography business now runs itself. They're adding marketing for a real estate client (branding, property content, virtual tours) and plan to use tools like Higgsfield plus scheduling automations for ~80% of the work. AI automations for the client may come later.
- **Everyone needs just one AIOS.** Several get messy and harder to sync. Use projects and drill-downs inside one.
- Nate's main AIOS has an "Other Worlds" folder, and each world is like its own mini AIOS. The top level is the router. It holds the skills, main instructions and assets, plus rules like "book questions go to this folder, cert program questions go to that one."
- **Routing is what makes it work,** because Claude has to find the needle in the haystack.
- Gut check: open the folder in File Explorer. Can you find a file without searching? If the structure doesn't make sense to a human, it won't work for Claude.
- To focus, open Claude Code in (or `cd` into) a subfolder. The root still has context of everything.
- One repo is also easy to sync. Push it to GitHub and pull it on a laptop, instead of juggling eight repos.
- **Follow-up: What if the client later wants access to their part?** Push just their section to its own GitHub repo.

### 13. Jared: Do you keep client development repos inside your AIOS, or do you sync them separately?
- First ask who else needs visibility. A personal AIOS and a team AIOS are very different problems.
- Shared knowledge bases need people (or their agents) responsible for pushing updates, in Google Drive or GitHub, and a way for everyone else to pull them.
- Team Claude plugins that update for everyone automatically are a good example. Most things don't work that way.
- A quote Nate liked: if humans are responsible for syncing, your agents aren't really productive. "They're more so just measuring human consistency." The real challenge is changing habits.
- **Keep development projects separate from your AIOS.**
- His team is testing a setup where everyone works on local files. An automated polling feature pushes updates to everyone and locks files while someone edits them. It's very experimental and buggy.
- **For now:** store projects in the cloud (ClickUp, Google Drive), pull local copies to work on, and push them back when a PR or eval is done.
- If you have a team, agree clearly that everyone keeps the source of truth updated. People need to nail the process before you can automate it.
- The goal is something like a shared Google Doc (see who's in it, everyone contributes, role-based access) but for GitHub repos and local files. Nate expects this to be solved within a year.

## Key takeaways
1. **Use the top model as an orchestrator.** Let it plan and route work to the cheapest model that can do it. High effort is the sweet spot, and Codex makes a cheap second reviewer.
2. **Consulting will consolidate and commoditize.** The moat is niche process expertise, governance and evals, and security and model redundancy are growing needs.
3. **Beginners:** build tools you use yourself, explain every decision clearly, then show proof through content or a portfolio. Do warm outreach before cold, and chase your first 10 nos.
4. **Fix your #1 constraint first.** Finish current commitments before a learning sprint, and pick a structured course over a quick challenge.
5. **Run one AIOS** with clear routing and folders a human can navigate. Push a client's section to its own repo when they need access.
6. **Tools:** Claude Code over Claude on the web, Claude Design for prototypes, and Codex with computer use or Playwright CLI for software with no API. Price that extra complexity into the scope.
7. **Stay focused.** Complementary expansion within a niche is fine, but big pivots are costly distractions.
8. **Team sync is a habit problem first.** Keep dev repos separate from your AIOS, use one cloud source of truth, and be clear about who pushes and pulls updates.
