## Q&A

### 1. Merlijn: Should I replace Customers.ai with my own chatbot built in Claude Code?
- Context: Merlijn pays for Customers.ai (formerly MobileMonkey) but barely uses it because it was hard to set up.
- For a chatbot on your own website or platform, Nate would "100%" not use a tool like Customers.ai. Building a SaaS product is a different conversation.
- **Build vs buy:** buy if you're in a time crunch (such as an investor pitch next week) and need a quick proof of concept. Otherwise build: it's cheaper long term, you own it, and the IP raises your company's value.
- The catch: only build when you know what "good and done" looks like. Without a clear outcome you're "building the plane as you fly it" and wasting resources (for example, a custom CRM built without knowing the integrations or data fields).
- A chatbot is a safe build: no big migration and no team upskilling.
- **Follow-up: My current chatbot drives visitors away. Should I build one into what I'm already making in Claude Code?** Yes, as long as it doesn't pull you away from higher-value founder work.
- **Follow-up: Are website chatbots still worth it?** Let the data decide. If you get lots of traffic but few conversions, a chatbot may help. Back your gut with data.

### 2. Ryan: What offer makes sense for a manufacturing consultant adding AI? Should I mention AI in cold outreach?
- Context: 20 years in manufacturing and operations (Fortune 100), 200+ companies audited, now a consultant and fractional operator. Cold email hasn't landed yet.
- AI positioning helps in some industries and conversations but not others. In 10 years nobody will call themselves an "AI consultant". Every consultant will have to be AI-native.
- **Lead with proof, then sprinkle AI on top.** His track record (tens of millions saved for companies) shows he speaks the buyer's language.
- Position yourself as the expert who knows how AI helps safely and, more importantly, when not to use it.
- Many buyers feel pressure from their board or competitors and want to "buy relief". Many have been burned by agencies that delivered a fancy demo or dashboard that does nothing.
- A line like "I doubled revenue in this business unit for a company like yours" gets a click. "New AI things" doesn't. Clients care about money, time and growth, not how you get there.
- **Follow-up: Does a lightweight "profit leak audit" work as the offer?** Yes. Nate thinks of offers as a ladder with four rungs:
  - First rung: sell hours for training or education. It's the lowest risk, and many owners will pay ~$100 for a one-hour session.
  - Cheap audits: low hundreds to low thousands.
  - Audits often surface automation wins that need no AI at all. In manufacturing, getting data AI-ready or automation-ready is a big early lift.
  - Let the audit fee count as a deposit on the first project, so scoping is paid either way.
  - Move to a retainer only after the client has felt the ROI.
- Give the audit a **named framework** (such as a four-step "Profit Leak Audit" run the same way every time). It makes deliverables consistent and sets you apart from people who have "just watched a bunch of YouTube tutorials".
- **Follow-up: Should I also offer to build specific tools, like a weekly ERP view of late suppliers?** Yes. Offering "want me to build one of these for you?" proves you're a specialist, not a generalist.

### 3. Christine: Any Claude or Codex tips for organizing photos and videos?
- First decide how you want them organized (by date, color, etc.).
- Have Claude, Codex or both run **screenshot verification loops**: analyze each image with vision, sort it into a bucket, and optionally have the other AI check the result.
- For videos, some GitHub repos extract frames and review them one by one.
- Keep a database alongside: one record per file with a unique ID, the date it was moved and a short description. Later reorganizations then don't need to redo the vision work, which saves time and tokens.
- **Follow-up: Where should the database live?** Google Sheets, Supabase or wherever you're comfortable. Nate uses Google Sheets for many internal databases because agents work with it easily.
- **Follow-up: Is OCR the loop?** OCR recognizes and extracts text in images. Vision understands what's in the picture. Both give AI "eyes".

### 5. Cheryl: Can an agent create Facebook content and post it in Facebook groups?
- Nate has little Facebook experience but sees no reason it's impossible. Blotato can post to Facebook. The rules for group posts may differ from regular posts.
- Have Claude or Codex research which APIs exist. If there's no API, computer use or browser use can do it.

### 6. Ed: How can AI help me promote a niche, data-rich site as an affiliate?
- Context: a horse racing data site with a large database. Ed writes racing articles, has a social following in the niche, and wants to drive paid sign-ups through articles, posts and teasers.
- It's a great AI use case. With the right content system, research and ideation are rarely the problem.
- The real challenge is making posts that are valuable, accurate and make people want to visit the site.
- Look at your best-performing posts and check whether they drive link clicks.
- Draw in your target avatar with a pain point or curiosity, and give enough value that it doesn't feel like a promotion.
- Train the system on your voice and the brand's voice so it doesn't sound like AI slop.
- **Track every article:** clicks, time on page and, most importantly, clicks on the CTA or affiliate link. An article with 100 views and 70 clicks beats one with 10,000 views and 4 clicks. Optimize for conversions, not top-of-funnel views.
- Let AI do the grunt work. Your job is reviewing, prompting for variety and setting strategy, as if you had hired a copywriter, a strategist and a data analyst.
- **Follow-up: Articles get passed around informally, which is hard to track. How do I handle that?** Attribution is tough. Also think about AEO/GEO: ranking in ChatGPT and Claude results, not just Google, by making articles stand out so an AI agent pulls them for users.

### 7. Jeffrey: How can I collect public data from various sources more reliably?
- Context: headless browsers and prompts work for some sources but not others.
- Use dedicated scrapers. The right one depends on the data and where it lives:
  - **Scrapling** (a GitHub repo): can get past some blocks to grab public data that Claude's web search or web fetch struggles with.
  - **Apify**: "actors" built for specific data sources. One may already exist for yours.
  - **Firecrawl**: crawl and scrape tools that may beat a generic approach.
- Nate's process: give Claude or Codex the ~5 URLs or platforms you scrape every week and ask it to research what tools already exist for them. Test what comes back, then formalize the winners in your scraping stack.

### 9. Yury: What are your best practices when a client needs HIPAA compliance?
- Nate's team has deliberately stayed away from HIPAA. Their one HIPAA project, a voice agent, was "pretty brutal".
- **Decide first whether you want to specialize in healthcare.** It's a huge edge because most people avoid it, but there's almost no reason to do it just once. If you won't specialize, he'd probably skip the project.
- If you do specialize:
  - Invest in a HIPAA lawyer to get you up to speed, possibly on retainer.
  - Work closely with the client's internal compliance people.
- How they protected themselves on that deal:
  - The contract put responsibility for compliance on the client. Anything going into production needed the client's written approval.
  - The client named an internal expert who worked closely with the team.
  - Lawyers helped write the contracts.
- **Split the responsibilities:** the client knows HIPAA and you know the AI side. Be able to explain where data goes, how long it's stored, when it's deleted, what's logged, what they're paying for and what goes into the system. Help set things up locally if needed.
- Expect longer sales and scoping cycles, internal approval of every API, and possibly on-premise setups.
- **Follow-up: Upwork and Fiverr only show cybersecurity specialists. Do I need an actual HIPAA lawyer?** Yes, one who has specialized in or has experience with healthcare and HIPAA.
- **Follow-up: How do I tell what is and isn't covered by HIPAA for a medical client?** You own the end-to-end data picture. The client's in-house compliance staff handle the HIPAA side, so collaborate closely and protect yourself in the contract.

### 11. Cecil: My knowledge base is full of fabrications and hallucinations. Should I start over or fix it?
- Context: a knowledge base for preparing debates, lectures and presentations.
- Make it pull exact sources every time. With good metadata it can quote a passage and say where it came from, which builds trust.
- When you get an answer you know is wrong, make it walk backwards: what it did, where it searched, and what it found where.
- Hallucinations have four main causes: bloat, confusion, clash and missing information (instead of flagging something as missing or outdated, the AI makes it up). You have to know which one it is to fix it.
- **Diagnose before you fix.** Ask lots of questions about where answers come from.
- **Follow-up: It started as an LLM wiki over ~200 books, and synthesis let fabrication creep in and made provenance harder. What now?** Work back from the end state. Wikis are good for showing relationships and following an index. For questions across many books, semantic search with a vector database may fit better. LLM wikis don't scale as well.
- **Follow-up: It got messy past 200 documents, and I then turned it into a legal knowledge base with strict citation standards. Fix or restart?** The "unsexy answer" is to build your own benchmark.
  - Create a **golden dataset** of hand-picked questions with known correct answers (200, for example).
  - Run every change or database design through it to see objectively which gives the highest accuracy.
  - Grow it over time. It's an eval, and it's the only way to say "this is better" instead of "this feels better".
- **Follow-up: I built one, but the agents faked it by looking at the eval. What then?** Isolate the eval from the agents.

## Key takeaways
1. **Build when you know the outcome.** Buy only for speed. Owning the build is cheaper long term and adds IP.
2. **Lead with proof, not AI.** Domain results open doors and AI is the add-on. Climb the offer ladder: paid hours, a named audit (fee as deposit), projects, then retainers.
3. **Content for clients vs growth:** pain-point titles for a specific buyer convert. News and new tools grow a small channel fastest.
4. **Measure what matters:** track CTA and affiliate clicks over views, and judge knowledge bases with a golden-dataset eval the agents can't see.
5. **Diagnose AI errors before fixing them.** Require cited sources and metadata, trace wrong answers back, and pick a wiki or semantic search based on the use case.
6. **Automate platforms carefully.** Research APIs and policies before automating Facebook groups. Drafts get you 95% there, and existing scrapers (Scrapling, Apify, Firecrawl) beat ad-hoc prompts.
7. **HIPAA is specialize-or-skip.** If you take it on, get a HIPAA lawyer and put compliance responsibility on the client in the contract.
8. **Stretch your AI budget:** Claude subscriptions stay inside Claude's own tools, and pairing Claude Max with a Codex plan gives more inference per dollar. For n8n to Trigger.dev migrations, convert with Claude Code and test in parallel.
