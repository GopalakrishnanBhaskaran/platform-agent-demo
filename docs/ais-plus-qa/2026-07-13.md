### 1. Mir: Why would the same automation find ~78 errors in the Claude Code desktop app but only 4–5 in VS Code?
- Context: an automation that edits legal transcripts (such as depositions) to the right legal style. Mir built it twice with the same markdown and reference files.
- **They shouldn't differ.** Both run the same harness and the same model under the hood.
- Look for other variables first: a different prompt, a different project, or a VS Code extension that's older or newer than the desktop app.
- AI is non-deterministic (ask 100 times, get 100 different answers), but a gap this big is concerning.
- This is why you test your skills and evaluate your agents: to make the output as deterministic as possible.
- **Follow-up: What if everything really is identical?** Mir said the instructions, reference files and prompt all match, and the gap is consistent. Check the versions, then post the details in the community for more ideas.

### 2. Josue: How do I land my first clients (warm or cold outreach), and what should I build as a proof of concept?
- **Automate as much of your own business as possible.** You become your own living case study, which earns trust faster than a demo.
- To design something valuable before anyone pays you, do market research on pain points or your own primary research (interviews).
- If you're going the consultant route, don't build a product to hand out unless you're heading toward SaaS. Automate your own work, and maybe friends and family.
- **Outreach ladder:**
  - Start making content now, knowing it takes a while for the algorithm to pick it up.
  - At the same time, do warm outreach. Build a big list (phone, email, Instagram, Facebook) and contact about 20 people a day. Start conversations and ask who they know, rather than selling.
  - Offer a free first project in exchange for a case study and a testimonial.
  - Once the list is exhausted, move to Upwork. Bid as early as possible with hyper-personalized proposals, including a ~2-minute Loom video (camera on) showing something similar you built.
  - Only then try cold email. Cold calling is the last resort.
- John ran an Upwork workshop at AIS Live. Upwork is much easier than cold outreach if you use it right.

### 4. Jason: How should I run a paid AI discovery project for a mid-market company? Any templates or workflows?
- There's discovery-call training in the AIS+ course. Also watch the sales call Nate and Pat ran at AIS Live.
- Open every discovery call by finding out why they booked: a specific moment of pain (often curiosity) or outside pressure (they need a solution now).
- Context: the 2–3 week paid audit is already sold to a home services company. Finance visibility and data analytics are a focus, and Claude training will likely follow.
- **Work in 3-month sprints.** Even for an audit, the deliverable should be "if we work together, here's what we'd do in the next 90 days."
- If they're open to it, start with **leadership training**. Culture flows down from leadership.
- Leave with about **3 big projects**, each with **one clear metric**. Everyone on their side should agree that moving it (say, from 10 to 20) in three months counts as success.
- Keep it simple, and expect to interview many people. Jason plans a kickoff with 4–5 leaders, then department and leader interviews.
- Nate's lesson: he built an executive assistant for an owner whose pain was "feeling busy". Two months in, the owner felt he wasn't getting value, because owners always feel busy. Agree on the metric up front so you both have "the receipt".

### 5. Danan: How do you keep track of everything you've built and what context each agent and automation has?

### 8. Girish: Should a three-developer client team use an enterprise plan, team/Max subscriptions or a local model?
- Context: a warm lead's developers are building an agency CRM, and Girish owns the AI strategy, starting with an infrastructure proposal. His idea: Claude (Fable) as orchestrator, Codex as the dev team.
- Nate has little hands-on experience with enterprise plans. **Focus on delivering value**: upskill the team on the tool you know best and scale from there.
- If they have a specific reason for a team plan, use it. Nate noted team seats top out at the $100/month tier, while personal plans go up to $200/month.
- Teach **model routing**: save Fable for the right tasks and use Sonnet workers elsewhere. That gets far more out of the usage.
- **Skip local models (like GLM) for now.** They aren't there yet unless you have massive hardware, which most businesses don't.
- Start with one tool and get the three engineers comfortable before adding more.
- **Follow-up: The budget is tight. Start on Pro and upgrade later?** Agree on a deliverable and a definition of done for a **30-day sprint**. Three Max plans cost about **$600 for 30 days**, and three people actively using them can build almost anything in that time. Your job is keeping them aligned on the deadline and working in the same codebase without stepping on each other.

### 9. Juan: How do I set up Fable as orchestrator for a phased build, and verify critical parts when I'm not an engineer?
- Context: a solo, real-time B2B auction platform on Next.js and Supabase, starting from the client's HTML prototype. The critical parts are concurrent bidding and data isolation.
- **Spec first.** Use the Grill Me skill to spec every element, but not with Fable (a waste of its intelligence).
- Write down everything you imagine could go wrong. Those are your edge cases.
- Then have Fable attack the spec: find the unknown unknowns and how to design against them. That gets the PRD about 90% there; the real world will still find bugs.
- **Make Fable the project manager.** It doesn't implement anything. It orchestrates Opus and/or Sonnet workers.
- Go phase by phase. Deliver each milestone and fully test it (verification loops, dynamic workflows) before starting the next.
- **Use Codex as the reviewer**, via the plugin or the CLI. It will almost certainly find bugs Opus and Fable missed. Fable then has Opus fix them.
- Don't move to the next phase until Codex and Opus both sign off.

### 10. Kierra: What's your tech stack, and how do you decide which tools to add, retire, build or buy?
- Nate's stack is broader than most because he experiments for videos. The core: **Claude Code connected to everything**, ClickUp for communication, Gmail and Google Workspace (Docs, Drive, Meet), and Fireflies for meetings.
- **Adding and retiring:** start from pain and keep the stack lean. First check whether something you already use, or Claude Code with a different prompt, can do the job.
- Try a new tool only if it solves a current pain. Skip viral tools you won't use day to day. Switching costs usually aren't worth it unless the long-term upside is big.
- **Build vs buy (how he frames it for clients):**
  - Buy (e.g. a $20/month SaaS): works on day one, proven, auto-updates, no maintenance, but may not fit perfectly.
  - Build: takes a few months, needs constant maintenance ("spinning plates"), and you don't know what you don't know.
- **Buy first**, for example for 3 months. What the team loves and hates about the product becomes your best requirements doc for a custom build, with less scope creep.
- **Follow-up: Do you use a platform for posting content and monitoring comments?** Just the YouTube dashboard. If he cross-posted everywhere, he'd build a custom dashboard. He built a YouTube dashboard early on, never used it, and forgot its domain.

### 11. Pedro: How do you grow or systematize a custom offer, and how should a scalable product be priced?
- Ask what the barrier to entry is and how fast you want your first dollar.
- **Services:** first dollar comes faster, but growth is linear and tied to your time.
- **Products:** first dollar comes slower (this used to require raising capital, but now you can build it yourself), and growth can be exponential.
- One person could realistically make $500K–$1M a year on 3–4 retainers at $20–30K/month, but it takes a lot of time, and a month off means lost income.
- To break the time cap: build a team, or move to **profit share, equity or performance-based deals**.
- Custom work is still viable. Nate left True Horizon for the mental headspace, not because it was struggling. McKinsey, Bain and BCG still run on projects, retainers and sometimes profit share.
- **Follow-up: How do I price a validated AI analytics product when usage varies a lot by client?** Nobody knows up front, so test what people will pay. Offer several pricing models and see which ones stick.
- Example: $50/month might get 100 signups who churn within months, while $25/month gets fewer who stay longer.
- Watch acquisition cost and **LTV, the one metric to focus on**. Decide between usage-based pricing and a capped monthly fee based on that. The $100M Offers and $100M Money Models books are good reading.
- Trust your gut, but trust the data blindly once you have it.

## Key takeaways
1. **Prove it on yourself first.** Automate your own business, then work the ladder: content plus ~20 warm contacts a day, a free project for a case study, Upwork with Loom proposals, and cold email last.
2. **Tie every engagement to one agreed metric.** Run discovery as a 90-day roadmap with about 3 projects, each with a KPI the client signs off on.
3. **Make a sprawling AI system visible.** Use one master folder, a log of live automations, and Grill Me/Doctor audits. Keep client deliverables outside the main OS behind pointer files.
4. **Keep client teams' tooling simple.** Use the plan you know best (about $600 for three Max seats on a 30-day sprint), teach model routing, and skip local models for now.
5. **Verify, don't trust.** AI is non-deterministic, so test skills and evaluate agents. For builds, spec with Grill Me, run Fable as a PM over workers in gated phases, and use Codex as an independent bug-finder.
6. **Lean stack, buy before you build.** Add tools only for real pain. A SaaS's flaws become the spec for a custom build. Claude Code can replicate n8n, but n8n's visual interface still helps enterprises.
7. **Pricing and growth:** services are fast but linear. Break the cap with a team, profit share or equity, and price products by testing and maximizing LTV.
8. **Content and career:** build content while still employed. A full pivot to AI usually belongs on a new channel, and being the AI person at work is a valid path too.
